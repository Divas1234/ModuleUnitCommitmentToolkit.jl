# PCM Overlap-Criteria Analysis for Normal IEEE-118 and Extreme-Ramp Scenarios

## 1. Objective and Scope

This experiment compares rolling-horizon PCM simulations on the IEEE-118 test system under a normal load profile and an extreme-ramp load profile. The comparison focuses on two dimensions:

- Simulation accuracy, measured by total operating cost, load shedding, wind curtailment, and cost gap relative to the full-criteria reference.
- Computational performance, measured by total subproblem solve time, Julia memory allocation, and average overlap-window length.

The two scenarios are:

- Normal IEEE-118: restored from `data/backup_loadcurve_before_extreme_ramp_20260808_1919/data_118.xlsx`.
- Extreme-ramp IEEE-118: generated by `tools/pcm/generate_realistic_loadcurve.jl`, with 168 hours of stochastic load variation and strong ramping events. The maximum hourly ramp is approximately `3111.96`.

The tested overlap-criteria combinations are `NoOverlap`, `SteadyOnly`, `UnitOnly`, `RampOnly`, `Steady+Unit`, `Steady+Ramp`, `Unit+Ramp`, and `Steady+Unit+Ramp`.

Simulation runtime is reported using `SubproblemSolveTime_sec`. Offline training, steady-state mapping calibration, and local reference-solution generation are stored separately and are not counted as rolling-simulation solve time.

## 2. Main Findings

The overlap mechanism mainly improves rolling-horizon robustness in the normal-load case. Under extreme ramps, it becomes a key mechanism for reducing myopic boundary errors and improving downstream feasibility and economics.

In the normal IEEE-118 scenario, `NoOverlap` has a cost gap of `0.3045%`. This indicates that a 24-hour execution window can still produce a reasonably close result when the load curve is smooth, although boundary myopia remains visible. Overlap windows further improve accuracy at the cost of higher solve time and memory use.

In the extreme-ramp scenario, the `NoOverlap` cost gap rises to `3.6549%`, and load shedding increases from about `6.46 MWh` in the normal case to about `41.04 MWh`. Strong ramping events amplify rolling-boundary errors because a subproblem that cannot see ramp pressure beyond the execution window may choose a locally economical but future-inflexible commitment plan.

The physical role of overlap under strong ramps is therefore clear: it gives current commitment decisions enough look-ahead information to reserve ramping capability for the next few hours, instead of optimizing only the immediate execution window.

## 3. Physical Meaning of the Criteria

`T_steady` represents the accuracy impact of the previous commitment boundary on later subproblems. Boundary commitment states continue to affect future decisions through minimum up/down times, ramping constraints, startup/shutdown costs, and dispatch trajectories. As the overlap window grows, later periods gain more freedom to correct the boundary-induced deviation. `T_steady` identifies a window that is long enough for this influence to decay below a chosen tolerance without being overly conservative.

`T_unit` represents the time inertia of thermal-unit on/off states. For online units, it is the remaining minimum online time before shutdown is allowed. For offline units, it is the remaining minimum offline time before startup is allowed. This criterion captures the fact that slow and strongly time-coupled units cannot freely change state near a rolling boundary.

`T_ramp` represents net-load ramping pressure after the execution window. When load rises or falls rapidly in the next few hours, the current commitment must prepare sufficient ramping capability in advance. This criterion is the most direct response to extreme-ramp events.

The three criteria correspond to different physical mechanisms: boundary-impact decay, unit commitment inertia, and net-load dynamic pressure. The composite rule takes the maximum candidate window, which is transparent and conservative but also increases subproblem size.

## 4. Normal IEEE-118 Scenario

The normal scenario has smoother load variation, so boundary myopia is less severe. `NoOverlap` has only a `0.3045%` cost gap, showing that rolling simulation remains close to the reference when the system is not under strong ramping stress.

`Steady+Unit` and `Steady+Unit+Ramp` both achieve a `0.0000%` cost gap and are the most accurate combinations in this scenario. Their average overlap window is `5.43 h`, and memory allocation is about `14272 MB`, compared with about `9526 MB` for `NoOverlap`.

`Steady+Ramp` has a cost gap of `0.0214%`, very close to the full-criteria case, while using an average overlap of `4.57 h` and about `13453 MB` of memory. This makes it a strong compromise for normal operating conditions.

`RampOnly` performs less well in the normal scenario, with a cost gap of `0.8830%`. The likely reason is that the normal load profile does not contain severe ramping events, so the dominant errors are more related to boundary commitment states and time-coupled unit behavior than to net-load jumps alone.

## 5. Extreme-Ramp Scenario

The extreme-ramp scenario substantially increases the value of overlap windows. `NoOverlap` reaches a `3.6549%` cost gap, indicating that ignoring ramp pressure beyond the execution window causes material economic and reliability penalties.

`RampOnly` is highly cost-effective in this scenario. It reduces the cost gap from `3.6549%` to `0.1140%`, with an average overlap window of only `3.43 h` and subproblem solve time of `16.32 s`. This shows that the ramp criterion captures the main look-ahead need under strong ramping stress.

`Steady+Ramp` has the best overall behavior in the extreme-ramp run. Its cost gap is `-0.2702%`, average overlap is `4.43 h`, solve time is about `21.66 s`, and memory allocation is about `13334 MB`. The negative gap should not be interpreted as strict global dominance over the full-criteria reference; it is mainly caused by independent MILP search paths, integer tolerances, and the `MIPGap = 1.5%` setting. Operationally, the result indicates stable economic performance at a reasonable computational cost.

`SteadyOnly` also performs well in the extreme case, with a `-0.2205%` cost gap, confirming that boundary-impact decay is important. However, its solve time reaches `22.78 s`, and it lacks an explicit ramp-event interpretation.

`Unit+Ramp` performs poorly, with a `4.1851%` cost gap, even worse than `NoOverlap`. This suggests that unit dwell inertia plus ramp detection is not sufficient if boundary-state accuracy decay is omitted. A longer overlap can still propagate an unfavorable boundary commitment path if the steady-state criterion is missing.

## 6. Accuracy and Performance Tradeoff

Overlap windows reduce rolling-boundary error, especially under extreme ramps. In normal operation, overlap mostly refines economic accuracy. Under strong ramps, overlap directly affects whether the system can prepare enough ramping capability in advance.

The computational cost increases because longer overlap windows expand the time horizon, variables, constraints, and integer decisions in each subproblem. Memory allocation is about `9526 MB` for `NoOverlap`, while combined-criteria strategies usually require `13-14 GB`.

Runtime is not determined by overlap length alone. Different criteria can change commitment paths, boundary conditions, and MILP search behavior, so solve time is not perfectly monotonic. For example, `Steady+Unit+Ramp` can solve faster than `SteadyOnly` in the normal scenario due to solver search-path differences.

The best criterion choice should therefore balance accuracy gain and computational cost based on the operating scenario, rather than simply maximizing the overlap window.

## 7. Recommended Strategy

For normal IEEE-118 simulations, `Steady+Ramp` or `Steady+Unit` is recommended. Both achieve near-reference accuracy without always relying on the most conservative full-criteria rule.

For extreme-ramp simulations, `Steady+Ramp` is recommended as the main strategy. It combines boundary-impact decay with future ramp-pressure detection, which matches the physical mechanism behind strong-ramp rolling-horizon errors.

For a faster online approximation, `RampOnly` is a useful option. In the extreme-ramp scenario, it removes most of the no-overlap error using a small average overlap window.

`Steady+Unit+Ramp` is best used as a conservative reference case. It has strong physical interpretability but higher computational cost, and it is not always materially better than `Steady+Ramp`.

`Unit+Ramp` should not be used as the main strategy based on this experiment, because it can miss the decay mechanism of boundary-state accuracy and may produce poorer economic results.

## 8. Implications for Future Work

The experiment supports the central motivation for adaptive overlap windows: rolling-horizon boundary errors are strongly scenario-dependent, so fixed-window or no-overlap strategies cannot robustly handle all operating states.

Future work can replace the current maximum-rule combination with a data-driven criterion-selection policy. Useful features include net-load ramp magnitude, the difference between prior commitment state and reference boundary state, remaining minimum up/down time, and predicted load-shedding risk.

The `T_steady` training dataset should also be expanded across more start periods, prior commitment states, and load-perturbation levels, so the boundary-impact decay model better reflects realistic rolling operations.
