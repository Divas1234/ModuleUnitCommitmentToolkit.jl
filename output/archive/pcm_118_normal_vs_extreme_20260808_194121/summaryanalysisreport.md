# Summary Analysis Report: Ordinary IEEE-118 vs Extreme-Ramp PCM Overlap Criteria

## 1. Experiment Scope

This archive compares rolling-horizon PCM performance on two IEEE-118 scenarios:

- Ordinary IEEE-118 load profile restored from `data/backup_loadcurve_before_extreme_ramp_20260808_1919/data_118.xlsx`.
- Extreme-ramp IEEE-118 load profile generated by `tools/pcm/generate_realistic_loadcurve.jl`, with 168 hours of stochastic load and strong ramp events.

The comparison uses eight overlap-criteria combinations:

- `NoOverlap`
- `SteadyOnly`
- `UnitOnly`
- `RampOnly`
- `Steady+Unit`
- `Steady+Ramp`
- `Unit+Ramp`
- `Steady+Unit+Ramp`

Operational simulation performance is measured by `SubproblemSolveTime_sec`. Offline training/calibration time and local reference-solve time are excluded from the operational solve-time metric and retained separately in the result CSV files.

## 2. Main Numerical Findings

In the ordinary IEEE-118 case, the no-overlap solution has a modest cost gap of `0.3045%` relative to the all-criteria reference. This indicates that when the load trajectory is relatively smooth, the short-sightedness of a 24-hour rolling execution window is present but limited.

In the extreme-ramp case, the no-overlap cost gap increases to `3.6549%`. Load shedding also rises from about `6.46 MWh` in the ordinary case to about `41.04 MWh`. This confirms that strong ramp events amplify the value of look-ahead information because the current commitment decision must prepare enough online capacity, ramp capability, and reserve margin for near-future stress.

The `RampOnly` criterion is highly effective under the extreme-ramp scenario. It reduces the cost gap from `3.6549%` to `0.1140%`, while increasing average overlap only to `3.43 h` and subproblem solve time from `14.96 s` to `16.32 s`. This shows that ramp-triggered overlap is the most direct low-cost mechanism for capturing strong net-load transitions.

The `Steady+Ramp` combination provides the best overall balance in the extreme-ramp case. It reports a cost gap of `-0.2702%`, average overlap of `4.43 h`, memory allocation of about `13334 MB`, and solve time of about `21.66 s`. The negative gap should not be interpreted as a strict proof that it globally dominates the all-criteria run; it is mainly a consequence of independent MILP solves, heuristic paths, and the `MIPGap = 1.5%` tolerance. Its practical meaning is that steady-state boundary sensitivity plus ramp anticipation is a robust and efficient overlap rule.

The `Unit+Ramp` combination performs poorly in the extreme-ramp case, with a cost gap of `4.1851%`. This suggests that unit dwell-time and ramp detection alone do not fully describe the downstream accuracy impact of a prior commitment boundary. Without the steady-state boundary-sensitivity term, a longer overlap window can still propagate a weak boundary state and produce inferior committed decisions.

## 3. Interpretation of Each Criterion

The steady-state criterion captures how much the prior commitment boundary continues to influence future subproblem accuracy. It is not just a time-length rule; physically, it represents the decay of boundary-condition dependence as the optimization horizon moves away from the previously fixed commitment state.

The unit-dwell criterion captures minimum up/down-time inertia. Its physical role is important for slow thermal units, because recently switched units cannot freely change status near the rolling boundary. However, by itself it does not know whether the upcoming load trajectory is benign or stressed.

The ramp criterion captures near-future net-load stress. It becomes especially important when strong upward or downward ramps occur just beyond the execution window. This is why its performance gain is much larger in the extreme-ramp case than in the ordinary case.

The combined criteria work by taking the maximum required overlap among active components. This is conservative and interpretable, but it can also increase memory and solve time. The results suggest that not every combination is automatically better; the selected criteria must match the dominant physical driver of boundary error.

## 4. Accuracy and Performance Tradeoff

For the ordinary IEEE-118 case:

- `Steady+Unit` and `Steady+Unit+Ramp` achieve the best cost accuracy, both with `0.0000%` gap.
- `Steady+Ramp` is nearly equivalent in accuracy, with only `0.0214%` gap.
- `RampOnly` is not enough in the ordinary case, with `0.8830%` gap.
- Average overlap around `4.57 h` to `5.43 h` generally yields near-reference accuracy.

For the extreme-ramp case:

- `NoOverlap` is clearly insufficient, with `3.6549%` cost gap.
- `RampOnly` is a strong low-cost option, reducing the gap to `0.1140%`.
- `Steady+Ramp` gives the best observed economic result and maintains moderate average overlap.
- `Unit+Ramp` is unstable in this test and should not be used as the primary policy without the steady-state term.

Memory usage increases mainly with overlap length. The no-overlap runs allocate about `9526 MB`, while combined-criteria runs generally allocate `13-14 GB`. Thus, overlap improves accuracy but expands the effective MILP horizon and the branch-and-bound search space.

## 5. Recommended Policy

For ordinary IEEE-118 operating simulation, `Steady+Ramp` or `Steady+Unit` is sufficient. They provide near-reference accuracy without relying on every criterion.

For strong-ramp operating simulation, `Steady+Ramp` should be treated as the preferred policy. It captures both boundary sensitivity decay and near-future ramp pressure, while avoiding the extra complexity of including unit dwell time in every interval.

For a low-cost online approximation, `RampOnly` is attractive in extreme-ramp conditions. It has much lower average overlap than the full criterion and still removes most of the no-overlap error.

`Steady+Unit+Ramp` should remain as a benchmark or conservative reference rather than the default policy. Its accuracy is good, but its extra solve time and memory are not always justified.

## 6. Research Implications

The experiment supports the core motivation for adaptive overlap windows: fixed or zero overlap cannot reliably handle operating periods with strong temporal coupling. The value of overlap is not uniform; it depends on the physical driver of future infeasibility or suboptimality.

Strong ramp events mainly require ramp-aware look-ahead. Boundary-state uncertainty requires steady-state sensitivity modeling. Slow-unit inertia requires dwell-time tracking. A practical adaptive PCM controller should therefore keep all three signals available, but choose an operational subset based on scenario stress and computational budget.

The next useful improvement is to convert the current max-rule combination into a calibrated decision rule. For example, the controller could learn when `RampOnly` is sufficient and when `Steady+Ramp` is needed, using features such as net-load ramp magnitude, prior commitment mismatch, remaining up/down-time pressure, and predicted load-shedding risk.
